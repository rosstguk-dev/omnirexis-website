Most gyms don't have a lead problem. They have a reply problem. Someone asks about joining, the question lands while you're coaching, and by the time anyone looks at it the moment has passed. Nobody decided to ignore them. It just slipped.

This guide sets out a simple follow-up process an independent gym can run without new software or extra staff: what to say in the first reply, a short follow-up sequence the gym owns, who should own it when the inbox is shared, and which parts are worth automating.

We haven't included conversion rates or "leads lost" figures. Plenty get shared online, but we couldn't trace the ones we found to a source we'd stand behind, so we've left numbers out.

## Why gym leads go cold

Leads rarely go cold because the person lost interest overnight. They go cold because of how a small gym's day works:

- **The reply waits while you're on the floor.** Enquiries arrive during classes, PT sessions and the busy evening hour, which is exactly when nobody can answer them.
- **Enquiries come in through several doors.** A phone call, a website form, an email to the info@ inbox. Each one lands somewhere different, and there's no single list of who's waiting.
- **Follow-up lives in someone's head.** One person remembers to chase, and when they're off or busy, nobody does.
- **One reply and done.** Someone gets a reply, doesn't answer straight away, and is never contacted again.

None of this is a marketing problem. You've already done the hard part by getting someone interested. The fix is a process that doesn't depend on memory.

## The first reply: what to say and what to ask

The first reply has one job: make it easy for the person to take the next step. It doesn't need to sell the gym.

A good first reply:

- **Uses their name and yours.** "Hi Sam, it's Jo from the gym" reads like a person, not a system.
- **Answers the question they actually asked.** If they asked about prices or class times, tell them, or tell them where to find it.
- **Asks one easy question.** "What are you hoping to work on?" or "Would mornings or evenings suit you better?"
- **Offers one clear next step.** A visit, a taster session or a quick chat at a time that suits them.

For example:

*Hi Sam, it's Jo from the gym. Thanks for getting in touch about joining. Our off-peak membership covers weekdays until 4pm, and evening classes are on the timetable here. What are you hoping to work on? If it helps, pop in for a look around this week and I'll show you the place.*

Reply as soon as you reasonably can. If you can't reply properly straight away, a short holding reply from a person ("Thanks Sam, I'm coaching until 7, I'll reply properly tonight") is better than silence, as long as you then do it.

## A follow-up sequence the gym owns

Most people who don't reply aren't saying no. They're busy, or they meant to reply later and forgot. A short, polite sequence gives them a couple more chances without nagging.

Here's a simple one to adapt:

1. **Day 0: the first reply.** As above. Answer the question, ask one thing, offer one next step.
2. **Next day: a short nudge.** *"Hi Sam, just checking my message reached you. Happy to answer anything else, or book you in for a look around."*
3. **End of the week: a helpful last message.** *"Hi Sam, I'll leave it here so I'm not filling your inbox. If you'd like to come in for a look around later on, just reply to this and I'll sort it."*

Then stop. Three messages over a week is enough for most enquiries. Anyone who says no thanks, or asks you not to contact them, should not hear from you again.

Keep each message short, personal and easy to answer in a few words. Use the channel they used to contact you where you can, so a phone enquiry gets a call back and an email gets an email.

A note on the rules: replying to someone who asked you a question is not the same as sending them marketing. If you later want to add enquiries to a marketing list or send them offers, check [the ICO's guidance on electronic mail marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/) first.

## Who owns follow-up when the inbox is shared

A shared info@ or hello@ inbox is where good enquiries go to wait. Everyone can see it, so everyone assumes someone else has replied.

A few rules fix most of this:

- **One named owner for new enquiries each day.** Not "the team". If that person is off, the job moves to someone else by name.
- **One list of open enquiries.** Every enquiry, whether it came by phone, form or email, goes on the same list with the date it came in and what happens next.
- **A clear status for each one.** For example: new, replied, followed up, booked a visit, joined, not interested.
- **A quick daily check.** The owner looks at the list once a day and clears anything that's waiting.

The list can be a spreadsheet to start with. What matters is that phone enquiries get written down too, because they're the ones most likely to vanish.

## What to automate and what to keep human

Some parts of follow-up are repeatable and easy to forget. Others need a person. It helps to split them.

**Worth automating:**

- Logging every enquiry from your web form and email in one place, so nothing depends on someone copying it across.
- Sending the next follow-up when an enquiry has gone unanswered for the time you've chosen.
- Putting the list of open enquiries in front of the right person each day.
- Stopping the sequence as soon as someone replies or books.

**Keep human:**

- Phone calls and the conversations that follow.
- Answering real questions about the gym, injuries, goals or anything unusual.
- Deciding when someone isn't a good fit, and saying so kindly.

Automation is there to take the remembering off your team. It shouldn't pretend to be a person, and the messages it sends should still read like they came from your gym.

## A simple daily follow-up routine

If you only take one thing from this guide, make it a routine you can keep:

1. **Every morning:** the named owner checks the list of open enquiries.
2. **Same morning:** anything new gets a first reply, and anything due gets its next follow-up.
3. **After the busy hour:** phone enquiries from the day are added to the list.
4. **End of the week:** close off anyone who has had all three messages, and note who booked or joined.
5. **Once a month:** look at where enquiries came from and where they stalled, and change one thing.

Small, regular and owned by a person. That's what stops good enquiries going cold.

## FAQ

### How quickly should a gym follow up a lead?

As soon as you reasonably can, ideally the same day. If you can't reply properly straight away, send a short holding reply from a person and then follow through. What matters most is that every enquiry gets a reply and a follow-up if they go quiet.

### How many times should I follow up a gym enquiry?

For most enquiries, three messages over about a week is enough: the first reply, a short nudge the next day and a helpful last message at the end of the week. Then stop, and stop straight away if anyone says no thanks.

### What should a gym's first reply to an enquiry say?

Use their name and yours, answer the question they asked, ask one easy question and offer one clear next step, such as a visit or a taster session. Keep it short and easy to answer.

### Who should follow up leads in a small gym?

One named person each day, not the whole team. Give them a single list of open enquiries from every channel, a clear status for each one and a few minutes every morning to clear it.

### Can follow-up for gym leads be automated?

The repeatable parts can, like logging enquiries from your web form and email in one place, sending the next follow-up when someone hasn't replied and stopping when they do.
